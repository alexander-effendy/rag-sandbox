# Dietary Fiber and Gut Health

*Synthetic document written for RAG testing. Named studies and figures are fictional and should not be treated as health information.*

## How Much Fiber

Most adults in industrialised countries consume between 15 and 18 grams of fiber per day. The Kestrel Cohort (2020), which tracked 12,400 adults across eleven years, identified 38 grams per day as the intake associated with the lowest all-cause mortality in its population.

The relationship was dose-dependent up to that point and then flattened. Participants consuming 38 grams or more showed a 22 percent lower rate of cardiovascular events than participants in the lowest quintile, but no further improvement was observed beyond roughly 45 grams.

A more practical formulation from the same authors scales fiber to energy intake: 14 grams of fiber per 1,000 calories consumed. This avoids under-recommending fiber to larger individuals and over-recommending it to smaller ones.

## Soluble and Insoluble Fiber

Fiber is not a single substance, and the two broad categories do different jobs.

Soluble fiber dissolves in water to form a gel. This slows gastric emptying, blunts the post-meal glucose curve, and binds bile acids, which is the mechanism behind its cholesterol-lowering effect. Oats, barley, psyllium, legumes, and apples are the common sources.

Insoluble fiber does not dissolve. It adds bulk to stool and speeds transit through the colon. Wheat bran, nuts, seeds, and the skins of most vegetables are the main sources.

Most whole plant foods contain both. The Kestrel authors noted that isolated fiber supplements reproduced only a fraction of the mortality benefit seen from whole food sources, and suggested the remainder came from the wider matrix of compounds that accompany fiber in real food.

## Short-Chain Fatty Acids

The most interesting fiber is the fraction that reaches the colon undigested and is fermented by gut bacteria. This fermentation produces short-chain fatty acids, principally butyrate, propionate, and acetate.

Butyrate is the primary energy source for colonocytes, the cells lining the colon. A colon starved of fermentable fiber is quite literally a colon starved of fuel, and this is the leading explanation for the association between low-fiber diets and intestinal barrier dysfunction.

Resistant starch is a particularly effective butyrate precursor. It occurs naturally in green bananas and cooked-then-cooled potatoes, rice, and pasta. The cooling step is not incidental: it drives retrogradation, in which gelatinised starch recrystallises into a form that digestive enzymes cannot break down. Reheating does not fully reverse this.

## Increasing Intake Safely

Moving from 15 grams to 38 grams per day too quickly reliably produces bloating, cramping, and gas. The gut microbiome adapts, but it adapts over weeks rather than days.

The Kestrel authors recommended increasing intake by no more than 5 grams per week, and increasing water intake alongside it. Insoluble fiber without adequate fluid worsens constipation rather than relieving it, which is the opposite of what most people increasing fiber are trying to achieve.
