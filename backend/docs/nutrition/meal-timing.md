# Meal Timing and Eating Windows

*Synthetic document written for RAG testing. Named studies and figures are fictional and should not be treated as health information.*

## Time-Restricted Eating

The Okonkwo Trial (2022) randomised 420 adults with metabolic syndrome to either a 10-hour daily eating window or unrestricted timing, holding total calories constant across both groups.

After twelve months, the restricted-window group showed a 14 percent improvement in fasting insulin and a 9 percent reduction in visceral fat despite the matched calorie intake. Body weight differed by less than one kilogram between groups, which the authors emphasised as the central finding: the benefit appeared to come from timing rather than from the calorie reduction that usually accompanies time-restricted eating in uncontrolled settings.

Windows shorter than 10 hours produced no additional metabolic benefit in the trial and were associated with substantially worse adherence. Only 51 percent of participants assigned an 8-hour window were still following it at twelve months, against 78 percent of the 10-hour group.

## Early Versus Late Windows

Where the window sits appears to matter more than how long it is.

The Okonkwo Trial included a sub-arm comparing an early window of 8am to 6pm against a late window of 12pm to 10pm, both 10 hours long. The early group showed roughly twice the improvement in fasting insulin.

The explanation offered is circadian: insulin sensitivity follows a daily rhythm and is highest in the morning, declining through the evening. An identical meal eaten at 8pm produces a measurably higher and more prolonged glucose response than the same meal at 8am. Melatonin, which rises in the evening, directly suppresses insulin secretion, and eating during that rise means eating with a blunted insulin response.

## Meal Frequency

The claim that frequent small meals raise metabolic rate does not survive controlled testing.

The thermic effect of food scales with the total amount eaten, not with how many sittings it is divided into. Six 400-calorie meals and three 800-calorie meals produce the same total thermic effect from the same 2,400 calories.

Frequency does matter for other reasons. As noted in the protein literature, distributing protein across three to four meals produces better lean mass outcomes than concentrating it in one or two, because each meal clearing the leucine threshold triggers a separate synthesis event. Satiety also differs between individuals in ways that are not well predicted by any measured variable, and the Okonkwo authors recommended treating frequency as a matter of adherence rather than metabolism.

## Eating Before Sleep

Eating close to bedtime is associated with worse metabolic outcomes and worse sleep quality, and the two effects appear to be partly independent.

Okonkwo participants who consumed their final meal within two hours of sleeping reported measurably worse sleep quality and showed higher overnight glucose. Digestion raises core body temperature, and a falling core temperature is one of the physiological signals that initiates sleep, so a large late meal works against the transition.

The practical guidance the authors settled on was to finish eating roughly three hours before bed, and to prefer an earlier eating window over a longer one where the two conflict.
