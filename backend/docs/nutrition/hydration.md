## Estimating Fluid Needs

The familiar advice to drink eight glasses of water per day has no clear origin in the literature and does not scale with body size, activity, or climate.

The Aldridge formula offers a better starting point: 35 millilitres of total fluid per kilogram of body weight per day. For a 70 kilogram adult this works out to roughly 2.45 litres. The formula describes total fluid, not water specifically, which matters because food contributes meaningfully to the total.

Roughly 20 to 30 percent of daily fluid intake comes from food in a typical mixed diet. Fruits and vegetables are the largest contributors, with cucumber, watermelon, and lettuce all exceeding 95 percent water by weight. Coffee and tea count toward the total despite their mild diuretic effect, which is more than offset by their fluid volume.

## Signs of Inadequate Intake

Thirst is a lagging indicator. It typically appears once fluid losses reach 1 to 2 percent of body mass, by which point cognitive and physical performance have already measurably declined.

Urine colour is the most practical field measure. Pale straw indicates adequate hydration; dark amber indicates a deficit. The main confounder is riboflavin from B-complex supplements, which turns urine bright yellow regardless of hydration status.

Performance effects begin earlier than most people expect. A fluid deficit of 2 percent of body mass is associated with a measurable decline in endurance capacity, and deficits above 3 percent begin to impair strength and reaction time.

## Sodium and Sweat

Water alone is not the whole picture, particularly during prolonged sweating.

Sweat sodium concentration varies widely between individuals, from approximately 200 to 2,000 milligrams per litre, with a population average near 900 milligrams per litre. This variation is largely genetic and is not meaningfully changed by training, though sweat rate itself does adapt.

Someone losing 1.5 litres of sweat per hour at the population average concentration loses roughly 1,350 milligrams of sodium per hour. For activity under an hour this is inconsequential and plain water is appropriate. Beyond two hours, particularly in heat, replacing sodium alongside fluid becomes important.

## Overhydration

Drinking too much is a real and occasionally fatal risk, and it is under-appreciated relative to dehydration.

Exercise-associated hyponatraemia occurs when large volumes of plain water dilute blood sodium below approximately 135 millimoles per litre. Early symptoms of nausea, headache, and confusion overlap almost exactly with those of dehydration, which is what makes it dangerous: the intuitive response of drinking more water makes it worse.

The condition is most commonly seen in slower marathon runners who drink at every aid station over four or more hours. The practical guard is to drink to thirst rather than to a schedule during prolonged endurance events, and to include sodium when events run long.

The kidneys can excrete roughly 800 to 1,000 millilitres of water per hour at maximum. Sustained intake above that rate is what creates the risk.
